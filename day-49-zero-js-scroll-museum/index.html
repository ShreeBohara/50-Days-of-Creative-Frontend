06

The Patient Reader

Catalogue essay — A short history of motion, without scripts

Movement arrived on the web wearing a costume. In 1996 the <marquee> element dragged text across the screen with the solemn dignity of a hotel lobby ticker, and for a decade that was roughly the state of the art. Anything more ambitious meant scripting — timers firing thirty times a second, elements nudged pixel by pixel, fans audibly objecting.

The stylesheet fought back slowly. Transitions appeared in WebKit in 2007, @keyframes shortly after, and suddenly a hover could bloom without a single callback. But the animations were deaf: they played on their own clock, indifferent to the one gesture the reader makes constantly and unconsciously — scrolling.

“Movement was never the property of the script tag; it was only ever on loan.”

So a generation of libraries grew up to translate. They listened to scroll events, throttled them, debounced them, measured bounding boxes and requested animation frames, all to answer a simple question: how far along is the reader? The answer powered every parallax hero and scroll-triggered reveal of the 2010s, and it ran, every frame of it, on the main thread.

Then in 2023 the question became a unit of style. animation-timeline: scroll() shipped in Chrome 115, and the scrollbar itself became a clock that any animation may keep. The browser drives it off the main thread, at the refresh rate of the display, and the page weighs nothing more than the stylesheet you are currently inside of.

The dial beside this essay is the proof. It knows how much of the chapter you have traversed because the chapter publishes its own position as a timeline — view-timeline: --chapter — and the dial subscribes with one declaration. The number in its centre is not typeset by a script; it is a CSS counter reading an animated @property integer. You may verify this claim at any speed you like.

conic‑gradient dial · the percentage is a CSS counter

Exhibit 06

The Patient Reader 2026

Cascade on essay — named view‑timeline, @property, conic‑gradient

The chapter declares view-timeline: --chapter; the sticky dial rides it through the contain range, animating a registered <integer> custom property from 0 to 100. The ring is a conic-gradient of that integer; the numeral is counter() reading the same value. Two properties, one timeline, zero script.

The Accumulation

  1. Acquisition I · 2023

    scroll( )

    The scrollbar clock. Chrome 115 taught animations to keep time by the reader’s thumb instead of the wall clock — the acquisition that made this museum possible.

    I

  2. Acquisition II · 2023

    view( )

    The element that watches itself. Every frame in this building knows exactly how far it has walked across your screen, without ever asking an observer.

    II

  3. Acquisition III · 2024

    @property

    The typed custom property. Numbers, integers, colours — once registered, the cascade animates them like any other value. The dial next door runs on one.

    III

  4. Acquisition IV · 2025

    Interop

    Safari 26 hung scroll-driven animations in its permanent collection; the technique stopped being a Chrome curiosity and became an architecture.

    IV

  5. Acquisition V · 2026

    Musée Zéro

    This page. Eight rooms, eight timelines, one stylesheet — hung without a single line of script and donated to the public domain of the scrollbar.

This exhibit has been running since you walked in. The plaster of every wall in the building is mixed from a single registered number — --hue — and your total scroll turns it once around the colour wheel, at a chroma so low you only notice when you look back at where you entered.
